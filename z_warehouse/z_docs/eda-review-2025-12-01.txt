## Chapter 1: Exploratory Data Analysis

**Lines:** 250 | **Sections:** 6

---

### 1. Core Concept

This chapter introduces EDA as a _mindset_ rather than a fixed set of techniques—contrasting Tukey's exploratory approach with confirmatory (model-based) analysis. The key message: let questions drive the analysis, remain open to unexpected patterns, and use visualization and summary statistics to understand data before modeling.

---

### 2. Key Skills

Students should be able to:

- Articulate the difference between exploratory and confirmatory analysis
- Use scatter plots, box plots, and histograms to examine distributions and relationships
- Compute and interpret basic summary statistics (mean, median, min, max)
- Formulate questions about an unfamiliar dataset
- Recognize "regression to the mean" as a phenomenon

---

### 3. Mathematical Prerequisites

Minimal:

- Basic descriptive statistics (mean, median, quartiles)
- Familiarity with scatter plots and histograms
- No calculus, linear algebra, or probability theory required

This chapter is accessible to students with limited mathematical background.

**Observations:**

- **Ordering issue**: The Tukey/EDA discussion (Section 4) comes _after_ two class exercises and a worked example. Students encounter "EDA" in practice before understanding what it is. Consider moving the conceptual discussion earlier—perhaps immediately after the opening example, or even before it.
- **Code visibility**: All code is hidden (`echo = FALSE`). For an introductory chapter, this may be appropriate to focus on concepts. However, if students are expected to _do_ EDA, they need to see how the visualizations are constructed. Consider showing code for at least one example.
- **Exercise depth**: The class exercises are very brief ("load data, what questions occur to you?"). Students new to EDA may flounder without more scaffolding. Consider adding guiding questions or a worked example of the question-formulation process.
- **The father-son example is effective**: It naturally introduces scatter plots, conditional distributions (box plots by father height), and the historical origin of "regression." This is a strong pedagogical choice.

---

### 5. EDA Connection

**Strong.** This chapter is explicitly _about_ EDA and models the exploratory mindset well. The father-son example demonstrates:

- Multiple views of the same data (scatter plot → grouped box plots → histogram of differences)
- Summary tables to quantify visual impressions
- Historical context (Galton, Pearson, regression to the mean)

**Opportunity**: The discussion of EDA vs. confirmatory analysis could be strengthened with a concrete example of how the same dataset might be approached differently under each paradigm.

---

### 6. Knox Handoff

This chapter sets philosophical groundwork but doesn't directly prepare students for specific ML methods. The connection could be made more explicit:

- EDA as the _first step_ in any ML pipeline (before feature engineering, model selection)
- The father-son data naturally leads to linear regression (Chapter 6)
- Diamonds data is often used for regression examples

**Suggestion**: Add a brief forward reference: "In later chapters, we'll see how EDA informs the choice of ML models and helps diagnose model failures."

---

### 7. Revision Notes

**High priority:**

1. **Restructure for clarity**: Move the "What is EDA?" discussion to Section 1 or 2, so students have conceptual framing before diving into examples.
2. **Show some code**: Reveal the R code for at least one visualization (e.g., the grouped box plot) so students see _how_ to do EDA, not just the results.
3. **Scaffold the exercises**: The class exercises are too open-ended for beginners. Add prompts like:
    - "How many levels does each categorical variable have?"
    - "What is the distribution of prices? Are there outliers?"
    - "Which variables might predict price?"

**Medium priority:**

4. **Connect to ML pipeline**: Add a paragraph explaining where EDA fits in the broader workflow (data → EDA → feature engineering → modeling → evaluation → interpretation).
5. **Add a "non-example"**: Show what _bad_ analysis looks like—jumping straight to modeling without exploring the data—to reinforce why EDA matters.

**Low priority:**

6. **Modernize resources**: The resource list is good but could include more recent references (e.g., the `skimr` package for quick summaries, or the `DataExplorer` package for automated EDA reports).

---

### Summary

Chapter 1 is a solid conceptual introduction that establishes the EDA philosophy through a well-chosen historical example. The main structural issue is that the _definition_ of EDA comes too late. For Masters students, the exercises need more scaffolding, and showing R code would better serve the practical goals of the course.
