## Chapter 4: Statistical Simulation — Review

**File**: simulation.qmd | **Lines**: 690 | **Sections**: 10+

---

### Core Concept

Statistical simulation as a computational tool for exploring systems with random components. The chapter progresses from basic random number generation through Monte Carlo methods to MCMC, positioning simulation as both an analytical tool and a foundation for Bayesian computation.

---

### Key Skills

- Use R's `d/p/q/r` distribution function families
- Design Monte Carlo experiments (mean vs. median comparison)
- Estimate quantities via rejection sampling
- Apply importance sampling to rare-event problems
- Understand Markov chain convergence to stationary distributions
- Implement the Metropolis algorithm
- Diagnose MCMC mixing via effective sample size (ESS)

---

### Mathematical Prerequisites

**Substantial.** Requires: probability density/mass functions, cumulative distributions, expected value, variance, conditional probability, logarithms, integral notation, basic Markov chain theory (transition matrices, stationarity), and comfort with mathematical derivations.

This chapter assumes more mathematical maturity than the "EDA" framing might suggest. The transition from Monte Carlo area estimation (accessible) to the detailed balance equations and Metropolis-Hastings derivation (graduate-level) is steep.

---

### Structure Assessment

**The chapter has scope issues similar to Chapter 2.** It covers at least three distinct topics that each deserve substantial treatment:

|Section|Lines|Content|
|---|---|---|
|RNG basics|56-142|Distribution functions in R|
|Monte Carlo|173-363|Area estimation, importance sampling|
|MCMC|365-644|Markov chains, Metropolis-Hastings|

The MCMC material alone (280 lines of dense mathematical derivation) could be its own chapter. The importance sampling example (Z ≥ 6 conditional expectation) is clever but potentially overwhelming for students still digesting the basics.

---

### Strengths

1. **Clear opening taxonomy** (lines 49-55): Four uses of simulation provides good framing
2. **Distribution function tables** (lines 106-142): Comprehensive reference for R's built-in distributions, with helpful historical notes (Beta-Gamma relationship, Poisson as limiting binomial)
3. **Mean vs. median variance ratio** (lines 144-168): Elegant example connecting simulation to known theoretical results (π/2 ratio). Good pedagogical choice—verify what we know before exploring what we don't.
4. **Monte Carlo π estimation** (lines 177-186): Classic example, well-executed
5. **Importance sampling motivation** (lines 256-276): The Z ≥ 6 example effectively demonstrates why naive rejection sampling fails for rare events
6. **Metropolis implementation** (lines 540-644): Complete, working code with δ-sensitivity analysis and ESS diagnostics. Students see the algorithm in action.
7. **Effective sample size** (line 662): Using `astsa::ESS()` introduces a practical diagnostic tool

---

### Weaknesses

1. **Code hidden throughout** (line 8: `echo = FALSE`): The Metropolis implementation code (lines 546-576) is excellent pedagogical material that students never see. This is particularly problematic for a chapter where _implementing_ the algorithms is the point.
2. **Abrupt mathematical escalation**: The chapter jumps from "here are R's distribution functions" to detailed balance equations and time-reversibility proofs. The MCMC section reads like graduate lecture notes dropped into an introductory EDA book.
3. **Orphaned content from Chapter 2**: The simulation table from Chapter 2 (lines 358-397 of `conditioning.qmd`) was flagged as misplaced. If that content was meant to move here, it hasn't been integrated—this chapter has its own RNG tables.
4. **Importance sampling underexplained**: The notation in equations 246-252 is introduced quickly. Students may struggle to connect the abstract weighted sum to the concrete Z ≥ 6 example.
5. **Missing EDA connection**: How does simulation serve EDA specifically? The chapter discusses simulation in general but doesn't connect back to the book's theme. When would an EDA practitioner reach for simulation?
6. **Typos/formatting**:
    - Line 65: "indpendent" → "independent"
    - Line 213: "Mote Carlo" → "Monte Carlo"
    - Line 530: "Generatecandidate" → "Generate candidate"
7. **Class exercise unscaffolded** (lines 169-172): "Generate samples, calculate mean and median, repeat R times, report variance" assumes students already know how to structure a simulation study.

---

### EDA Connection

**Weak.** The opening paragraph (line 49) mentions "assessing the robustness of data analyses" and "understanding system behavior," but these threads aren't developed. The chapter doesn't show simulation as an EDA tool for understanding _data_—it's more about understanding _methods_.

Possible connections that could be made explicit:

- Bootstrap resampling for uncertainty quantification in EDA
- Permutation tests for assessing whether observed patterns are meaningful
- Simulation to check model assumptions (simulate from fitted model, compare to data)

### Knox Handoff (ML Preparation)

### Overall Assessment

**This chapter is ambitious but overreaches.** The progression from distribution functions to Metropolis-Hastings in 690 lines is too compressed for most audiences. The MCMC material is well-written but belongs in a different book (or a different part of this book, after more mathematical preparation).

The strongest material is the mean vs. median comparison and the Metropolis implementation with δ-sensitivity analysis. These demonstrate simulation's power clearly. The importance sampling section is elegant but may lose students.

**Key question for revision**: Is MCMC essential for this course? If Knox's ML portion includes Bayesian methods, this foundation is valuable. If not, the MCMC section could be trimmed or moved to an appendix, making room for more EDA-relevant simulation applications (bootstrap, permutation tests).
