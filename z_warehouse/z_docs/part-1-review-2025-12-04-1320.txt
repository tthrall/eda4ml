That's the crux of the issue. A chapter that doesn't connect forward can feel like an intellectual detour to students, even if the material is intrinsically valuable.

A few observations:

**The case for keeping it despite no explicit reuse:**

Information theory _does_ pervade ML, even if your book doesn't foreground it. Cross-entropy loss is the workhorse of classification. KL divergence appears in variational autoencoders and regularization. Information gain drives decision tree splits. Mutual information is used for feature selection. A student who understands entropy and mutual information will recognize these ideas when they encounter them in specialized courses—they just won't see them again in _your_ book.

That's a defensible pedagogical choice: equip students with foundational concepts they'll need elsewhere, even if you don't build on them yourself.

**The structural risk:**

Part 1 would then have two distinct threads:

- Chapters 1, 2a, 3: visual/geometric EDA (distributions, conditioning, clustering)
- Chapter 2b: information-theoretic EDA (entropy, mutual information)

If Chapter 2b sits between conditioning and clustering, it interrupts the geometric flow. If it comes after clustering (as Chapter 4), it delays simulation and study design.

**A possible resolution:**

Position the information theory chapter as a _coda_ to Part 1—after study design, as Chapter 6. Frame it explicitly as "conceptual vocabulary for ML" rather than as a tool you'll use immediately. A brief forward-reference section at the end ("Where you'll see these ideas again: cross-entropy in Chapter X, decision trees in Chapter Y...") would justify its inclusion even if your book only gestures toward the applications.

Alternatively, if you're committed to Part 1 ending at Chapter 5, the information theory material could become an appendix that Chapter 2 references—available for those who want depth, but not blocking the main narrative.

What's your preference for Part 1's endpoint—five chapters or six?
